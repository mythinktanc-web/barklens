The house is quiet and the dog is not. Panting at 1 a.m. is something you notice, but the observation alone does not explain its cause.

Dogs may pant with heat, stress, excitement, or exertion. ([Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/recognizing-and-responding-canine-respiratory-distress)) Panting can also occur with medical conditions, including Cushing’s and some forms of heart failure. ([Merck Veterinary Manual: Cushing Disease](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/endocrine-system/the-pituitary-gland/cushing-disease-pituitary-dependent-hyperadrenocorticism-in-animals); [WSU Veterinary Teaching Hospital](https://hospital.vetmed.wsu.edu/2021/12/15/heart-valve-malfunction-in-dogs-mitral-insufficiency/))

Those are general possibilities, not a diagnosis for your dog. New or repeated night panting is something to discuss with your veterinarian rather than explain away from a webpage.

## What to bring to your veterinarian

Write down when the panting started and whether it occurs while your dog is resting or after activity. Note the room temperature, how long the episode lasts, and anything else you noticed at the same time.

Bring the medication labels, including prescribed steroids, pain medicines, or heart medicines, along with any known start dates or changes. Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine because of this article.

If your veterinarian asked you to record resting breathing rates, bring those records and follow the method they taught you. Do not use an internet cutoff to change treatment, and do not delay care to take a measurement when your dog is having trouble breathing.

## What the house already knows

Clock time. Whether your dog was asleep, resting, or moving around. Changes in thirst, appetite, sleep, or willingness to walk. Keep the observations dated.

Previous lab reports and medication history can give the veterinarian context. A symptom beside a flagged lab value does not establish a cause; the interpretation belongs with the examination and the rest of the record.

## At your next veterinary visit

Bring the dated night notes, medication labels, and any previous test results. Tell the veterinarian what changed without trying to decide whether the cause is heat, discomfort, a medicine, or an underlying condition.

Do not wait for a routine appointment if your dog is having difficulty breathing, develops blue or grey gums, or collapses; seek emergency care immediately. ([Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/recognizing-and-responding-canine-respiratory-distress)) If your dog cannot rest comfortably, contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic right away. ([WSU Veterinary Teaching Hospital](https://hospital.vetmed.wsu.edu/2021/12/15/heart-valve-malfunction-in-dogs-mitral-insufficiency/))

## Frequently asked questions

### Does panting at night mean heart failure?

Not by itself. Panting and breathing changes have more than one possible explanation, and a veterinarian must determine whether heart failure or another cause is involved. ([Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/recognizing-and-responding-canine-respiratory-distress); [WSU Veterinary Teaching Hospital](https://hospital.vetmed.wsu.edu/2021/12/15/heart-valve-malfunction-in-dogs-mitral-insufficiency/)) This article cannot rule heart failure in or out.

### Should I count breaths?

If your veterinarian has asked you to record resting breathing rates, follow their method and instructions. If you have not been given a plan, ask whether keeping those records would be useful; do not delay emergency care to count breaths.

### When is panting an emergency?

Difficulty breathing, visible abdominal effort, blue or grey gums, or collapse require immediate emergency veterinary care. ([Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/recognizing-and-responding-canine-respiratory-distress)) If you are unsure whether your dog is in respiratory distress, seek veterinary help rather than relying on this page to decide that waiting is safe. ([Cornell](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/recognizing-and-responding-canine-respiratory-distress))
