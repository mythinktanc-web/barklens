Liver enzymes in dogs

Four values on a routine panel describe the liver. Two point at liver cells, two at the bile system — and one of them moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the liver at all.

Where BarkLens fits

The medication list is the point here. So much is processed by the liver that “what is he currently on, at what dose, since when” is often the first question worth answering. The Rimadyl label itself says baseline blood and biochemistry values should be considered before, and periodically during, administration of any NSAID.

Source · Rimadyl label (DailyMed)

Enzyme height doesn’t tell you severity — direction tells you more. Labs & Trends puts every ALT and ALP on one line against the range printed on its own report, so a value that’s drifting reads differently from one that jumped once and came back.

Ask what a value means with the rest of the panel beside it. The AI Companion answers with his own results in view, so “is his ALP high” becomes a question about his GGT and bilirubin too.

Liver cells — ALT and AST

Cornell’s eClinPath states that ALT is a fairly liver-specific enzyme in dogs and cats. It sits inside liver cells and spills into blood when those cells are injured.

Source · Cornell eClinPath — ALT

AST is different. eClinPath states AST is not organ specific: skeletal muscle contains the highest concentration, followed by liver and cardiac muscle, and it is also present in red blood cells, kidney cells and brain tissue.

Source · Cornell eClinPath — AST

The value most owners have never heard of

If AST is raised and you need to know whether it’s muscle or liver, the answer is usually CK (creatine kinase) — a muscle enzyme, run alongside.

eClinPath notes that CK activity rises and falls quickly after muscle injury, while AST — with a longer half-life of around 24 hours in dogs — increases more gradually and stays raised longer.

Source · Cornell eClinPath — Creatine Kinase

So the timing of the two relative to each other is informative, which is another way of saying: one draw won’t tell you.

The bile system — ALP, GGT and bilirubin

ALP (sometimes printed ALKP) rises with obstructed bile flow — but also with steroids, dental disease, and normal bone growth in young dogs. It is the least specific value on the panel. A raised ALP alone frequently means nothing about the liver.

GGT is more specific for bile duct irritation. GGT rising alongside ALP makes the bile system a likelier explanation than ALP by itself.

Bilirubin usually moves last. eClinPath states that increases in bilirubin in dogs often occur after increases in the cholestatic enzymes GGT and ALP, and that in acutely developing jaundice ALP and GGT activity may still be normal because they require time to induce.

Source · Cornell eClinPath — Bilirubin

Which means the order in which values move carries information that no single report can show you.

What enzyme height does and doesn’t tell you

Height does not equal severity. A markedly raised ALT does not mean a worse problem than a mildly raised one, and a normal panel does not rule out liver disease. This is why vets often order a bile acids test or imaging rather than simply repeating the same enzymes.

Questions worth asking your vet

·Could a medication, dental disease, or his age explain this before we look at the liver?
·Would CK help tell muscle from liver here?
·Does the combination across all four change your thinking?
·Would a recheck in four to six weeks be more useful than imaging today?
