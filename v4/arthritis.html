Arthritis in dogs

There is no blood test for arthritis. That’s the most useful sentence on this page, so it goes first.

What you see at home is not a soft substitute for real assessment — it’s part of the assessment. The Merck Veterinary Manual states that the Canine Osteoarthritis Staging Tool characterizes an individual dog on a 0–4 scale based on risk factors, owner observations, physical examination findings, and radiographic results.

Weight, tracked consistently. ACVS states that a slender body weight is by far the most critical aspect of osteoarthritis management, because fat produces inflammatory mediators and increased body weight puts additional force on the joints. What bloodwork is actually for here

When your vet runs blood on a dog with suspected arthritis, they usually aren’t looking for arthritis. They’re checking whether pain medication is safe to give — kidney and liver values in particular, since many anti-inflammatories are processed there and monitored over time.

Bloodwork can also help rule out conditions that resemble arthritis but aren’t, including tick-borne infections and immune-mediated joint disease.

Why management involves so many moving parts

International consensus guidelines on canine osteoarthritis treatment state that optimal management is complex, requiring consideration and selection of multiple intervention options, both pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical.

Source · COAST consensus (Front Vet Sci)

Which in practice means several things at once — medication, weight, activity, sometimes supplements or physical therapy — each with its own schedule. That’s a record-keeping problem before it’s anything else.

What vets ask you about

Stiffness after rest. Hesitation on stairs or jumping. Slowing on walks. Difficulty rising. Licking at a joint. Changes in sleeping position. Reluctance on slick floors.

Dated notes and short videos are far more useful than a description from memory — and they’re exactly the kind of thing that’s easy to intend to record and then not.

One thing that isn’t a wait-and-see

The Merck Veterinary Manual’s when-to-see-a-veterinarian table lists swollen joints under “see a veterinarian within 24 hours.”

Source · Merck — When to See a Veterinarian

Questions worth asking your vet

·What should I be recording between visits?
·Would a staging tool like COAST be useful for him?
·Do his kidney and liver values allow this medication long term, and how often should we recheck?
·Is his weight where you’d want it, and what scale are you scoring him on?
·Could this be something other than arthritis?
·What would tell you the current plan isn’t working anymore?
