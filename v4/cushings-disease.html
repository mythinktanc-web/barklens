Cushing’s disease in dogs

Many owners land here because a routine panel showed a high ALP and someone mentioned Cushing’s. That’s a reasonable thing to ask about — and it is not what a high ALP means on its own.

Where BarkLens fits

What you notice at home is what prompts the test. Drinking more, urinating more, a bigger appetite, panting, coat changes. Dated notes and photos turn “he seems thirstier lately” into something with a start date.

Water is measurable. It’s one of the few things you can put a number on at home, and it’s central to this picture. Log it.

Keep glucose in view. Cornell’s eClinPath states that dogs with diabetes mellitus who become insulin resistant or need increasing insulin doses should be tested for underlying Cushing’s disease. Labs & Trends keeps both on one line, which is how a pattern like that becomes visible.

Source · Cornell eClinPath — Glucose

Why the routine panel isn’t the answer

ALP is commonly raised in dogs with Cushing’s. It’s also raised by steroids, dental disease, normal growth, and plenty of unrelated things. A raised ALP is a reason to ask a question, not an answer to one.

Cholesterol and triglycerides often rise too, and urine specific gravity is often low, because affected dogs drink and urinate more.

Merck explains why the urine is so dilute: high cortisol blocks the water-conserving hormone ADH from working.

Source · Merck — Cushing Disease

What actually tests for it

ACTH stimulation test — measures how the adrenal glands respond to a stimulating hormone.

Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test — measures whether cortisol suppresses the way it should.

Urine cortisol-to-creatinine ratio — the 2023 AAHA endocrinopathies guidelines describe this as a very sensitive but nonspecific test that may be considered to rule the condition out: a negative result makes the diagnosis very unlikely, but a positive result does not confirm it.

Source · 2023 AAHA Endocrinopathies Guidelines

None of these appear on a routine wellness panel. They’re ordered deliberately, because of a clinical picture.

Why timing of the test matters

The 2023 AAHA guidelines state that attempts should be made to stabilize or resolve any known comorbidities — such as diabetes mellitus — before pursuing Cushing’s testing when possible.

So “not yet” can be a clinical answer rather than a delay, and knowing that makes the conversation easier.

The two conditions push on each other: eClinPath notes that in dogs with Cushing’s disease, high blood sugar is caused by insulin resistance from excess corticosteroids.

Questions worth asking your vet

·Could a medication or dental disease explain the ALP before we look further?
·Which test would you use, and what would it rule in or out?
·Do the things I’ve noticed at home fit this picture?
·Should anything else be stabilized before we test?
·If we don’t test now, what would make you want to?
