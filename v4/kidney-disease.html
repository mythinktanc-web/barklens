Kidney values in dogs

Your vet mentioned his kidney numbers, or you’re holding a panel with BUN and creatinine on it. These values are read together, and with urine — almost never one at a time.

Kidneys have enormous spare capacity — enough to hide a problem for years. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that clinical signs of chronic kidney disease generally don’t appear until at least 75% of kidney function is gone.

Source · Merck Veterinary Manual

Where BarkLens fits

Every draw on one line. One kidney panel tells you where he was that day. The same values across three draws tell you which direction they’re moving — and the ACVIM consensus statement on hypertension lists serial increases in creatinine or SDMA as evidence of kidney damage, meaning the change over time is itself the finding.

Source · ACVIM consensus statement

Keep the medication picture beside the numbers. Merck’s guidance on ACE inhibitors recommends starting at the lower dose range and increasing with monitoring of kidney function, potassium, and blood pressure — three things that live on three different reports.

Source · Merck — ACE Inhibitors

What the markers measure

BUN (blood urea nitrogen) — a waste product the kidneys clear. It also rises with dehydration, a high-protein meal, or bleeding in the gut. On its own it’s a weak signal.

Creatinine — more specific to filtration than BUN, but affected by muscle mass. A heavily muscled dog and a thin one can produce different values with identical kidney function.

SDMA — tends to move earlier than creatinine and is less affected by muscle mass.

Urine specific gravity — how concentrated the urine is. This is why your vet asks for a urine sample alongside blood.

Why urine changes the reading

Cornell’s eClinPath states that an adequate urine specific gravity in an azotemic animal usually indicates a pre-renal azotemia — meaning raised waste products with concentrated urine point somewhere different from raised waste products with dilute urine.

Source · Cornell eClinPath

What’s read together

IRIS staging guidelines state that chronic kidney disease in dogs is staged initially on fasting creatinine or fasting SDMA or — preferably — both, and substaged on proteinuria and blood pressure.

Source · IRIS Staging Guidelines

So the full picture is usually four things, not one: a filtration marker, urine concentration, protein in the urine, and blood pressure. Phosphorus, calcium and potassium commonly join it.

Anemia belongs in this conversation too. Merck notes chronic kidney disease is a common cause of non-regenerative anemia, because erythropoietin is produced in the kidney, and a published study reports approximately 70% of dogs with CKD develop anemia as the disease progresses.

Source · Merck — Nonregenerative Anemias Source · Published study (PMC)

That’s why a red blood cell count on a page about kidneys isn’t a filing error.

Questions worth asking your vet

·Would a urine test help interpret these numbers?
·Could dehydration or his last meal explain this?
·Should we be checking blood pressure and urine protein as well?
·Would a recheck in a few weeks show whether this is steady or moving?
·Do any of his current medications affect these values?
